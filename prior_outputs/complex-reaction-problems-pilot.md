# Complex Reaction Problems — Pilot Set

Same underlying reactions as your alkanes/alkenes database — no new reaction types introduced. The molecules are bigger and combine multiple functional groups/rings so the *decision* of what reacts (and where) isn't automatic.

**One flag before you start:** Q8's ketone-vs-acid split under hot acidified KMnO4 depends on how substituted each alkene carbon is (no H → ketone; one H → acid). Your JSON only says the product is "carbonyl compound(s) and/or carboxylic acid(s)" without stating this substitution rule explicitly — it's a standard extension of that line, but check it against your lecture notes before trusting it.

For each question: given the molecule (name + condensed structural formula) and the reagents/conditions, predict the product(s). Try before looking at the answer key.

## Chemoselectivity — same reagent, is it substitution or addition?

**Q1.** Hex-1-ene, CH2=CH-CH2-CH2-CH2-CH3, reacted with Br2, in CCl4, room temperature, in the dark.

**Q2.** 2-Methylpentane, CH3-CH(CH3)-CH2-CH2-CH3, reacted with Br2, in CCl4, room temperature, in the dark.

## Benzene as a spectator — does the ring react?

**Q3.** 4-Phenylbut-1-ene, C6H5-CH2-CH2-CH=CH2, reacted with Br2, in CCl4, room temperature, in the dark.

**Q4.** 4-Phenylbut-1-ene, C6H5-CH2-CH2-CH=CH2, reacted with H2, Ni catalyst.

## Multi-site tracking — more than one reactive site of the same kind

**Q5.** Hexa-1,4-diene, CH2=CH-CH2-CH=CH-CH3, reacted with KMnO4, dilute H2SO4, heat.

**Q6.** Octa-1,6-diene, CH2=CH-CH2-CH2-CH2-CH=CH-CH3 *(double bonds at C1–C2 and C6–C7)*, reacted with Br2, in CCl4, room temperature, in the dark.

## Branching — the reactive site (and its substitution pattern) isn't at C1

**Q7.** 3-Methylpent-2-ene, CH3-CH=C(CH3)-CH2-CH3, reacted with KMnO4, dilute NaOH, cold.

**Q8.** 2-Methylbut-2-ene, CH3-C(CH3)=CH-CH3, reacted with KMnO4, dilute H2SO4, heat.

## Which carbon? — free-radical substitution isn't site-selective

**Q9.** 2-Chloropropane, CH3-CHCl-CH3, reacted with Cl2, UV light. *(Further chlorination — more than one product is possible.)*

**Q10.** Butane, CH3-CH2-CH2-CH3, reacted with Br2, UV light. *(More than one monobromination product is possible.)*

# Answer Key

*Try each question yourself first. Only read the trap explanation if you got it wrong.*

**A1.** CH2Br-CHBr-CH2-CH2-CH2-CH3 (1,2-dibromohexane). Electrophilic addition across the C=C only.
> *If you got this wrong:* Br2 is also the reagent for free-radical substitution on alkanes — but that needs UV light or heat to generate radicals. Here it's dark and room temperature, and CCl4 as solvent is the signature of electrophilic addition. Conditions, not the reagent alone, tell you the mechanism.

**A2.** No reaction.
> *If you got this wrong:* there's no C=C for electrophilic addition, and no UV light/heat to initiate free-radical substitution on this alkane. Br2 + alkane in the dark at room temperature simply doesn't react — a reagent being present doesn't guarantee a reaction occurs.

**A3.** C6H5-CH2-CH2-CHBr-CH2Br. Addition happens only at the alkene; the ring is untouched.
> *If you got this wrong:* benzene resists electrophilic addition (it would destroy the ring's stability) and simple Br2 without a catalyst like FeBr3 won't substitute onto it either. The ring is a spectator here — the alkene is the only reactive site.

**A4.** C6H5-CH2-CH2-CH2-CH3. The alkene is hydrogenated to an alkane chain; the ring is untouched.
> *If you got this wrong:* H2/Ni reduces C=C under these mild conditions, but reducing an aromatic ring needs much more forcing conditions than this reaction represents. Same "ring is a spectator" logic as Q3.

**A5.** CO2 + H2O (from the terminal C1=C2), plus HOOC-CH2-COOH (propanedioic acid, from the middle fragment), plus CH3COOH (ethanoic acid, from the other end).
> *If you got this wrong:* there are two independent C=C bonds, and hot acidified KMnO4 cleaves both. Track each double bond separately: the terminal one goes fully to CO2 + H2O, the internal one splits into two carbonyl-turned-acid fragments joined through the middle CH2.

**A6.** CH2Br-CHBr-CH2-CH2-CH2-CHBr-CH2Br-CH3 (1,2,6,7-tetrabromooctane).
> *If you got this wrong:* both double bonds react independently with the addition reagent — it's easy to spot and fix only one C=C and stop there.

**A7.** CH3-CH(OH)-C(OH)(CH3)-CH2-CH3 (3-methylpentane-2,3-diol).
> *If you got this wrong:* the double bond is between C2 and C3, not at the start of the chain — cold dilute KMnO4 adds OH to both of those carbons regardless of the branch sitting on C3.

**A8.** (CH3)2C=O (propanone) + CH3COOH (ethanoic acid).
> *If you got this wrong:* the two alkene carbons aren't equivalent. C2 carries two methyl groups and no H, so it can only be oxidised to a ketone (no H left to remove for further oxidation to an acid). C3 carries one H, so it goes all the way to a carboxylic acid.

**A9.** Two valid products: ClCH2-CHCl-CH3 (1,2-dichloropropane) and CH3-CCl2-CH3 (2,2-dichloropropane).
> *If you got this wrong (or only gave one):* free-radical substitution can abstract any C–H, not just one adjacent to the existing Cl. The radical doesn't "know" where the first substitution happened.

**A10.** Two valid products: BrCH2-CH2-CH2-CH3 (1-bromobutane) and CH3-CHBr-CH2-CH3 (2-bromobutane).
> *If you got this wrong (or only gave one):* butane has two chemically distinct sets of hydrogens (the four primary H's on C1/C4, and the four secondary H's on C2/C3). Free-radical substitution doesn't pick a "favourite" carbon — both types of C–H are reactive.
